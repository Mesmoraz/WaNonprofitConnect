What we're building

We're building a map to see the golden thread between nonprofits, businesses, and people.

Every nonprofit is a pin where it actually is. When one organization helps another, a thread appears between them. Zoomed out you see where help is flowing across the state; zoomed in you see who helped whom, with what, and when.

Around the map is the part that makes the threads happen: a board where organizations say what they need and what they have, matching that finds who fits, and a private conversation to work out the hand-off. The next page walks through each piece.

How it works

Here's each piece, where we're at with it, and what's still missing.

The public listEvery nonprofit is already written down. We start from there.

Every nonprofit is registered with the IRS. The IRS publishes that registry as a free spreadsheet anyone can download. It lists each organization's name, address, tax ID, what kind of organization it is, and roughly how big it is.

The nonprofits do not need to sign up for this service. We use the IRS's database to pull in that information so there's no extra work on their part. We found that Washington has 35,361 nonprofit organizations, and all of them are available in our app from day one. Someone who works at one of these organizations will be able to "claim" it in the app, as long as they can verify they work there.

MissingPhone numbers, emails, and websites. The IRS spreadsheet doesn't have them. We'll have to find that information from other sources — other public filings, the state's charity registry, or the organization itself once it's claimed.

Keeping the list currentThe IRS updates monthly. So do we, without touching anything a person typed.

Where we're atThe IRS reposts the spreadsheet every month. We built a small program that downloads the new one, compares it to what we have, and updates only what changed: new organizations get added, changed ones get updated, ones that dropped off get flagged rather than deleted. It writes down every run so we can always say how fresh the data is.

It never overwrites anything a person entered. If a food bank writes its own description, that stays theirs no matter what the next IRS file says.

MissingRight now someone has to run it. We still need to put it on a schedule so it runs itself each month, and to pull in the IRS's other monthly files — the one that says who's currently in good standing, and the one that says who lost their status.

Where everything livesOne place for the organizations and everything people add.

Where we're atAll 42,510 Washington organizations are loaded and searchable, along with the needs and offers people post, the connections between them, and the messages inside those connections. Every organization is filed under its IRS tax ID, so the same one can never show up twice.

MissingAccounts. Today the prototype pretends you're signed in. The real version needs real sign-in, and a record of which people belong to which organizations.

The rulesOne front door that every screen goes through, so the rules can't be bent.

Where we're atThe phone app, the website, and anything we build later never touch the stored data directly. They all go through one door and ask: search organizations, post this need, accept this connection. The door checks who's asking and whether the rules allow it, then does the work.

That means the rules live in one place. Only the other side can accept a connection. Only the two organizations in a connection can read their messages. An organization can only have two "we can't operate without this" needs open at a time. No screen can get around any of that.

MissingKnowing who's really asking. Today a request just says which organization it's for and we take its word. Real sign-in and "prove you work there" are the next thing to build.

The appThe screens: board, map, organizations, post, inbox, you.

Where we're atA clickable prototype you can open on a phone. It walks through the whole story — a food bank posts a need, a refurbisher gets alerted, they connect, message, and hand off — from both sides. The organizations in it are real; the people, posts, and messages are invented so we could show the flow.

MissingThe real thing. The prototype keeps everything on your device and pretends to sign you in. The finished app will be the same screens, connected to the real data, with real accounts.

Being told the moment something happensYou shouldn't have to keep checking.

Where we're atWhen a need is posted that matches what you have, when someone accepts your proposal, when a message arrives — the app is told the instant it happens rather than the next time you refresh. Public news (a new listing) goes to everyone. Private news (your connections, your messages) goes only to the two organizations involved. This works today.

MissingReaching you when the app is closed: email and phone notifications, and a daily digest for the less urgent stuff.

The mapWhere help is, and where it's flowing.

Where we're atEvery organization is placed by its ZIP code. Nearby ones gather into numbered circles when you're zoomed out and spread apart as you zoom in. Every connection is a thread; completed ones are gold. You can filter by needs or offers, who's giving, category, and distance. Page 6 explains how to read it.

MissingStreet-level placement (we know the addresses; we just haven't turned them into exact map points yet), and a real street map underneath instead of the plain outline.

From a need to a hand-off

Six steps. Both sides can always see where things stand.

1

You post

"We need twelve laptops." Or: "We have forty sheets of plywood." You pick a category and a quantity, and for a need, how urgent it is.

2

We look for fits

We compare it with everything open and let the organizations that fit know — including ones that haven't posted anything but told us "we often have laptops."

3

Someone raises a hand

One side taps "I have this" (or "I need this"). That pairs the need with the offer and asks the other side.

4

The other side says yes

Only they can. Once accepted, both listings are held so nobody else can claim them in the meantime.

5

You work it out

A private conversation for just the two of you: timing, pickup, who to call. Nobody else can see it.

6

Done

Either side marks it complete. Both listings close, and the hand-off shows on both organizations' pages and on the map as a gold thread.

If the other side says no, nothing changes. If you cancel after accepting, both listings just reopen.

A few things you'll run into

How urgent: P0 to P3So "urgent" keeps meaning something.

P0 — we can't operate without it. P1 — we need it this week. P2 — this month. P3 — whenever it works.

We limit each organization to two open P0s a month. Otherwise everything becomes a P0 and the word stops helping anyone.

Things that repeatWeekly produce. Monthly bookkeeping. A standing arrangement.

A need or an offer can be one-time, weekly, monthly, quarterly, or ongoing. When a repeating pair is accepted it becomes a standing agreement: it has a next date, each delivery gets logged with one tap, and either side can end it whenever. "Monthly produce to Thurston County Food Bank since July — three deliveries so far."

Who can see whatNeeds and offers are public. Conversations aren't.

Needs and offers are meant to be seen — that's the whole point. Connections and messages are private to the two organizations in them. The IRS file lists a contact person for each organization; we never show that name.

Matching & fairness

We always show our work, and everyone gets a turn at the top.

How we decide who fits

For a need, every open offer gets points, and we show the points. Ballard Food Bank needs twelve laptops; Computers 4 People in Need has fifteen:

Same kind of thing (Technology)+50 Same words: laptop, computer+35 Different cities+0 The need is a P0+10 The offer covers the whole amount+10 The offer is recent+5 Match score110

Weak fits don't get shown. And an organization gets told about a fit even if it hasn't posted anything — as long as its page says "we often have laptops."

MissingLearning from what actually gets completed. Right now the points are fixed; over time we'd like the ones that lead to real hand-offs to count for more.

How we keep the board fair

A board that rewards whoever is biggest or posts the most would quietly bury the small organizations. So we don't do that.

Most urgent first, then least-seen

Among needs of the same urgency, the one fewer people have seen goes to the top. It rotates.

No crowding

No organization gets more than two of the top six spots.

A reserved seat

One of the top spots always goes to the listing that's been seen the least, whoever posted it.

We say why

Every listing says why it's where it is ("P1 · seen twice"). Every organization can see how often it's shown compared with average.

Not for sale

If we ever show sponsored content, it's labeled and it sits outside this list. Placement can't be bought.

Alerts follow rules, not size

You hear about something because it fits what you said you need or have, near you. Never because someone is big or popular.

Reading the map

Pins are organizations. Threads are help.

Green pin — a nonprofit, placed at its ZIP code.

Blue pin — a business that gives.

Purple pin — a person who gives.

7

Numbered circle — several organizations close together. Tap it and they spread apart. The soft glow behind it is how active that place is.

Gold thread — a finished hand-off between two organizations.

Green thread — accepted, being worked out.

Amber dashed — someone raised a hand; waiting on the other side.

Grey dotted — we think these two fit; nobody has acted yet.

Which way is it going?

Threads sit quietly until you pick one. Tap a thread — or choose it from the Connections list — and it lights up: the dashes drift and a small dot travels along it from the giver to the receiver. Everything else fades back so you can't lose the one you chose.

Filters

Needs or offers, who's giving, what kind of thing, and how far from you — plus which kinds of thread to show. The same filters run the Board, so the list and the map always agree.

For builders

The technical page. If you're not building on this, you already have the whole story.

This documents the backend as it exists today. In the language of the earlier pages: "the rules" is an API, "where everything lives" is a SQL Server database, "being told the moment something happens" is SignalR.

Every endpoint

An endpoint is one web address with one job. GET reads, POST changes something. Reading organization data is open; changing anything requires naming the organization you act for (today a header X-Org-Ein; later a real login). Development address http://localhost:5253; interactive documentation at /swagger.

Organizations (public data)

GET/api/v1/orgsSearch. Filters: q (words, or a 9-digit EIN), city, zip, ntee (K or K31), foundation, only501c3, excludePrivateFoundations, minRevenue/maxRevenue, page/pageSize.
GET /api/v1/orgs?q=food+bank&city=Seattle
GET/api/v1/orgs/{ein}One organization, every IRS code translated to words, with ProPublica and IRS links. Never includes the "in care of" name.
GET/api/v1/statsCounts by cause, size, city, filing type.
GET/api/v1/nteeThe 26 cause categories.
GET/api/v1/codesEvery IRS code table, translated.
GET/api/v1/importsThe import log: file, date, fingerprint, rows changed.
GET/health"Healthy" when the database is reachable.

Listings (needs and offers)

GET/api/v1/listingsThe board. Filters: kind, category, status (default Open), city, orgEin, q.
POST/api/v1/listingsPost a need or offer as the acting organization. Alerts everyone whose listings or tags fit.
POST /api/v1/listings (X-Org-Ein: 911428805) { "kind":"Need", "category":"Technology", "title":"Laptops for client intake stations", "quantity":12, "unit":"laptops", "urgency":"Critical", "tags":"laptops,computers" }
GET/api/v1/listings/{id}One listing.
GET/api/v1/listings/{id}/matchesBest counterparts, each with a score and plain-English reasons.
POST/api/v1/listings/{id}/closeThe poster takes it down.

Connections and messages

GET/api/v1/matchesThe acting organization's inbox, with threads.
POST/api/v1/matchesPropose: needListingId, offerListingId, optional note. You must own one side.
GET/api/v1/matches/{id}One connection with its thread. Parties only.
POST/api/v1/matches/{id}/acceptThe other side accepts; both listings become Matched.
POST/api/v1/matches/{id}/declineThe other side declines; listings stay open.
POST/api/v1/matches/{id}/completeEither side; both listings become Fulfilled.
POST/api/v1/matches/{id}/cancelEither side; an accepted pair reopens.
POST/api/v1/matches/{id}/messagesSend a message. Not on declined or cancelled connections.

Errors are short sentences with a standard code: 400 invalid input, 401 you didn't say who you are, 403 not allowed ("Only the two organizations in this match can access it"), 404 not found, 409 wrong state ("Offer listing is Matched"). Every endpoint is rate-limited per caller.

Live updates

EventWho gets it
LIVElisting.created · listing.updatedEveryone connected
LIVEmatch.proposed / accepted / declined / completed / cancelledThe two organizations in the connection
LIVEmessage.sentThe two organizations in the thread

An app connects once to /hubs/exchange, says which organization it represents, and then just listens.

What is real and what is simulated

Real today

IRS data for all 42,510 Washington organizations in a database. The endpoints above, with matching, connections, messaging and live updates — run end-to-end by a command-line demo and 57 automated tests. The 32 organizations in the prototype are real records.

Simulated in the prototype

Sign-in, the people (Casey, Jordan), every listing, connection and message, the example businesses and community member, the alerts. The prototype runs the same matching rules in the browser and keeps everything on your device.

Designed, not built

Claiming an organization and verifying you belong, business and supporter accounts and payments, standing agreements and priorities in the backend, email and push alerts, the finished app. See the design brief.

Glossary

501(c)(3)
The part of US tax law for charities. Gifts to them are tax-deductible; the IRS decides who qualifies.
EIN
An organization's permanent nine-digit tax ID. Our primary key.
NTEE code
The cause category (K31 = food banks).
API / endpoint
The front door; each address on it is an endpoint with one job.
JSON
The text format requests and answers travel in.
SignalR
The technology behind live updates.
Listing
One need or one offer.
Match / connection
One need paired with one offer: Proposed → Accepted → Completed.
Standing agreement
An accepted recurring pair with logged deliveries.
ZIP centroid
The center of a ZIP code area; where a pin sits until street-level geocoding exists.
Acting organization
The organization a request is made for — today a header, later your login.
