The problem

Nonprofits need help. People and businesses want to give it. The two rarely find each other.

A food bank's intake computers die.

Nobody nearby knows. A refurbisher forty miles away has fifteen laptops sitting on a shelf.

A business has forty sheets of plywood left over.

It would love to give them away. It has no idea who could use them, or how to ask.

There's no shared place to look.

Who needs what, right now, near me? Who has extra? Who already helps whom? Today that lives in people's heads and inboxes.

How we're handling it

One place where every nonprofit is already listed, can say what it needs or has, and gets connected to the people who can help. The results show up on a map, so anyone can see help moving from one place to another.

The next pages walk through each part in plain language. The last page is for developers and can be skipped.

🗂️1 · The public listEvery nonprofit in the state, already written down.

Every nonprofit in the United States is registered with the IRS. The IRS publishes that registry as a free spreadsheet anyone can download — one file per state, refreshed every month. It lists each organization's name, address, tax ID, what kind of organization it is, and roughly how big it is.

We start from that spreadsheet. That is why no nonprofit has to sign up to be found: Washington's 35,361 charities are all there on day one. Someone who works at one of them simply claims it.

What the spreadsheet does not have: phone numbers, email, or websites. Those come later — from other public files, or from the organization itself once claimed.

🔄2 · The updaterKeeps our copy of the list current, without touching what people typed.

A small program that, once a month, downloads the newest IRS spreadsheet and compares it with what we already have. New organizations are added. Organizations that changed are updated. Ones that disappear from the file are flagged, never deleted, so history stays intact.

It writes down every run — which file, when, how many rows changed — so we can always answer "how fresh is this?". And it never overwrites anything a person entered: a description a nonprofit wrote stays theirs.

🗄️3 · The storageWhere everything lives.

Think of a set of linked spreadsheets that many people can safely use at the same time: one for organizations, one for needs and offers, one for the connections between them, one for messages. Later, one for accounts.

Every organization is filed under its IRS tax ID, so the same organization can never be listed twice.

🚪4 · The rulebookOne front door that every screen goes through — and the rules live there.

The phone app, the website, and any tool we build later never reach into the storage directly. They all knock on the same door and ask: "search organizations", "post this need", "accept this connection". The door checks who is asking and whether the rules allow it, then does the work.

Because there is one door, the rules exist in exactly one place: only the other side can accept a connection; an organization can hold at most two "can't operate" needs at a time; only the two parties can read a thread. No screen can bend them.

Developers call this door an API. The last page lists everything it can do.

📱5 · The appyou use thisThe screens: board, map, organizations, post, inbox, you.

What a nonprofit, a business, or a neighbor actually sees. Today it's the clickable prototype; the finished version will be the same screens on a phone and on the web. The app holds no rules of its own — it asks the door and shows the answer.

The six tabs: Board (needs and offers), Map, Orgs (every organization), + (post something), Inbox (alerts and your connections), Me.

⚡6 · Live updatesYou're told the moment something relevant happens.

Normally an app has to keep asking "anything new?". Instead, the door keeps a line open and pushes news the instant it happens: a need was posted that matches what you have, someone accepted your proposal, a message arrived.

Public news (a new listing) goes to everyone. Private news (your connections, your messages) goes only to the two organizations involved.

🗺️7 · The mapyou use thisWhere help is, and where it's flowing.

Every organization as a pin where it actually is. Every connection as a thread between two pins — gold once the hand-off is done. Zoomed out, nearby pins gather into numbered circles; zoom in and they spread out. Page 6 explains how to read it.

Where the organizations come from

The IRS already keeps the list. We just read it carefully.

For each organization, the IRS spreadsheet tells us a handful of things. Here is what they mean and why we care.

Tax ID (EIN)

A permanent nine-digit number, like a Social Security number for an organization. We use it as the one true identifier, so nobody can be listed twice. Shown like 91-1428805.

Name, address, ZIP

As registered. The ZIP code places the pin on the map — accurate to the neighborhood, not the front door.

Charity or not

The IRS marks which organizations are 501(c)(3) charities — the kind you can donate to tax-free. Only those can use the exchange as nonprofits.

Public charity vs. foundation

A food bank and a family foundation are both charities, but a foundation gives money and rarely needs plywood. We tell them apart.

Cause

A category code: food, housing, education, animals, and so on. About one in five organizations has none, and some are wrong — so organizations can fix their own tags.

Size

Yearly revenue and what kind of tax return they file. Most Washington charities are small enough to report no financial details at all.

What the list doesn't give us

Phone numbers, email, or websites. Those come from other public files (small organizations list a website on their yearly postcard to the IRS), from the Washington Secretary of State's charity registry, or — best of all — from the organization itself once someone claims it.

How it stays current

The IRS reposts the file every month. Our updater fetches it, changes only what changed, and never overwrites what a person typed. Every screen says which month's file it's showing.

From a need to a hand-off

Six steps, and both sides can see where they are.

1

Post

A nonprofit says "we need twelve laptops" — or "we have forty sheets of plywood". It picks a category, a quantity, and for needs a priority.

2

Match

The system compares it with everything open and alerts the organizations that fit — including ones whose profile simply says "we often have laptops".

3

Propose

One side taps "I have this" (or "I need this"). That pairs a need with an offer and asks the other side.

4

Accept

Only the other side can say yes. Once accepted, both listings are held so nobody else can claim them.

5

Talk

A private thread for the two of them: timing, pickup, a contact card. Nobody else can see it.

6

Complete

Either side marks it done. Both listings close, and the hand-off is recorded on both profiles and drawn on the map in gold.

If the other side declines, nothing changes. If an accepted pair is cancelled, both listings simply reopen.

A few words you'll see

Priority: P0, P1, P2, P3

P0 — we can't operate without it. P1 — needed this week. P2 — needed this month. P3 — whenever it works.

To keep P0 meaningful, an organization can have at most two open P0s a month.

Recurring needs and standing agreements

A need or offer can be one-time, weekly, monthly, quarterly, or ongoing. When a recurring pair is accepted it becomes a standing agreement: it has a next date, each delivery gets logged with a tap, and either side can end it at any time. "Monthly produce to Thurston County Food Bank since July, three deliveries so far."

Who can see what

Needs and offers are public — that's the point. Connections and messages are private to the two organizations in them. An organization's "in care of" contact from the IRS file is never shown.

Matching & fairness

Every match comes with its reasons. Every listing gets its turn.

How a match is scored

For a need, every open offer gets points, and the points are explained. Ballard Food Bank needs twelve laptops; Computers 4 People in Need has fifteen:

Same category (Technology)+50 Same words: laptop, computer+35 Different cities+0 The need is P0+10 The offer covers the whole quantity+10 The offer is recent+5 Match score110

Weak matches (under 15) are not shown. Organizations are also alerted by their own profile tags — "we often have: laptops" — even before they post an offer.

How the board stays fair

A board that rewards whoever is biggest or posts the most would quietly bury small organizations. So:

Priority first, then the least-seen

Within a priority level, the listing that has been shown to the fewest people rises to the top. It's a rotation, not a popularity contest.

No crowding

No organization holds more than two of the top six spots.

A reserved seat

One of the top spots always goes to the least-seen open listing, whoever posted it.

Say why

Every listing on the board says why it's there ("P1 · seen twice"). Every organization can see how often it's shown compared with average.

Not for sale

Sponsored content is labeled and lives outside this ranking. Placement can't be bought.

Alerts follow rules, not size

You're alerted because something fits your tags, category, and area — never because someone is big or popular.

The map

Where help is, and where it's flowing.

Green pin — a nonprofit, placed at its ZIP code.

Blue pin — a business that gives.

Purple pin — a community member who gives.

7

Numbered circle — several organizations close together. Tap it to zoom in and they spread apart. The soft glow behind it is how active that place is.

Gold thread — a completed hand-off between two organizations.

Green thread — accepted and in progress.

Amber dashed — proposed, waiting for an answer.

Grey dotted — suggested by matching; nobody has acted yet.

Reading direction

Threads are quiet until you choose one. Tap a thread — or pick it from the Connections list — and it lights up: the dashes drift and a small dot travels along it from the giver to the receiver. Everything else fades back so you can't lose it.

Filters

Needs or offers, who's giving, category, and distance from you — plus which kinds of thread to show. The same filters drive the Board, so the list and the map always agree.

For builders

The technical page. Everything the door can do, in one list.

If you're not building on this, you can stop here — the earlier pages are the whole story. This page documents the backend as it exists today.

Every endpoint

An endpoint is one web address with one job. GET reads, POST changes something. Reading organization data is open; changing anything requires naming the organization you act for (today a header X-Org-Ein; later a real login). Development address http://localhost:5253; interactive documentation at /swagger.

Organizations (public data)

GET/api/v1/orgsSearch. Filters: q (words, or a 9-digit EIN), city, zip, ntee (K or K31), foundation, only501c3, excludePrivateFoundations, minRevenue/maxRevenue, page/pageSize.
GET /api/v1/orgs?q=food+bank&city=Seattle
GET/api/v1/orgs/{ein}One organization, every IRS code translated to words, with ProPublica and IRS links. Never includes the "in care of" name.
GET/api/v1/statsCounts by cause, size, city, filing type.
GET/api/v1/nteeThe 26 cause categories.
GET/api/v1/codesEvery IRS code table, translated.
GET/api/v1/importsThe import log: file, date, fingerprint, rows changed.
GET/health"Healthy" when the database is reachable.

Listings (needs and offers)

GET/api/v1/listingsThe board. Filters: kind, category, status (default Open), city, orgEin, q.
POST/api/v1/listingsPost a need or offer as the acting organization. Alerts everyone whose listings or tags fit.
POST /api/v1/listings (X-Org-Ein: 911428805) { "kind":"Need", "category":"Technology", "title":"Laptops for client intake stations", "quantity":12, "unit":"laptops", "urgency":"Critical", "tags":"laptops,computers" }
GET/api/v1/listings/{id}One listing.
GET/api/v1/listings/{id}/matchesBest counterparts, each with a score and plain-English reasons.
POST/api/v1/listings/{id}/closeThe poster takes it down.

Connections and messages

GET/api/v1/matchesThe acting organization's inbox, with threads.
POST/api/v1/matchesPropose: needListingId, offerListingId, optional note. You must own one side.
GET/api/v1/matches/{id}One connection with its thread. Parties only.
POST/api/v1/matches/{id}/acceptThe other side accepts; both listings become Matched.
POST/api/v1/matches/{id}/declineThe other side declines; listings stay open.
POST/api/v1/matches/{id}/completeEither side; both listings become Fulfilled.
POST/api/v1/matches/{id}/cancelEither side; an accepted pair reopens.
POST/api/v1/matches/{id}/messagesSend a message. Not on declined or cancelled connections.

Errors are short sentences with a standard code: 400 invalid input, 401 you didn't say who you are, 403 not allowed ("Only the two organizations in this match can access it"), 404 not found, 409 wrong state ("Offer listing is Matched"). Every endpoint is rate-limited per caller.

What is real and what is simulated

Real today

IRS data for all 42,510 Washington organizations in a database. The endpoints above, with matching, connections, messaging and live updates — run end-to-end by a command-line demo and 57 automated tests. The 32 organizations in the prototype are real records.

Simulated in the prototype

Sign-in, the people (Casey, Jordan), every listing, connection and message, the example businesses and community member, the alerts. The prototype runs the same matching rules in the browser and keeps everything on your device.

Designed, not built

Claiming an organization and verifying you belong, business and supporter accounts and payments, standing agreements and priorities in the backend, email and push alerts, the finished app. See the design brief.

Glossary

501(c)(3)
The part of US tax law for charities. Gifts to them are tax-deductible; the IRS decides who qualifies.
EIN
An organization's permanent nine-digit tax ID. Our primary key.
NTEE code
The cause category (K31 = food banks).
API / endpoint
The front door; each address on it is an endpoint with one job.
JSON
The text format requests and answers travel in.
SignalR
The technology behind live updates.
Listing
One need or one offer.
Match / connection
One need paired with one offer: Proposed → Accepted → Completed.
Standing agreement
An accepted recurring pair with logged deliveries.
ZIP centroid
The center of a ZIP code area; where a pin sits until street-level geocoding exists.
Acting organization
The organization a request is made for — today a header, later your login.
